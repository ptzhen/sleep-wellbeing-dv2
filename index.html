Sleep and Wellbeing in Australia

We all know we should get 7 to 9 hours. A third of us don't. Follow one Australian night, from lights-out to sunrise, to see who's awake, what's keeping them up, and how it shows the next day.

1 · How we compare

On paper, Australians sleep well

Australia sits mid-pack among OECDOrganisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, a group of 38 mostly high-income countries. Its time-use data also covers a few non-members, such as China, India and South Africa. countries. On the OECD figures, which use Australia's 2006 time-use surveyPeople record what they do across a whole day in a diary, so sleep is added up from the diary rather than estimated from memory., Japan sleeps about 70 minutes less and Korea about 40 minutes less. Survey years and methods differ, so small gaps mean little.

Women sleep a few minutes longer than men in most countries. In Australia's 2024 survey the gap has all but closed.

Diaries count naps too, so these figures run higher than the hours people report in surveys.

8h 48m

Was your guess close? That is the average day's sleep for Australians aged 15 and over in 2024.
So why do a third of adults say they sleep less than 7 hours a night?

2 · A day in Australia

The weekday alarm clock steals sleep we try to win back at the weekend

Between 7 and 8 am, only 43% are still asleep on a weekday, against 60% at the weekend. That lie-in adds up to about 44 extra minutes a night (2024), a sign that many people run short during the week.

Press Play the night, or hover over an hour to see who is asleep.

On a weekday, 80% of workers have started by 9 am, the same hours in which sleep drops away.

Free time is squeezed into the evening, peaking at 75% between 8 and 9 pm.

Clocks set the working day, but the sun sets the body clock. With no daylight saving, Brisbane's summer sunrise is 4:49 am, 51 minutes before Sydney's.

3 · Who sleeps well

The young sleep longest and still feel the most tired

Young adults sleep the longest, just over 9 hours a day at 15 to 24 (2024). Yet they are the least likely to feel they get enough, and two in three often wake up unrefreshed.

Three in four of them are online in the last hour before bed most nights. Among people over 65 it's one in five.

New parents lose the most, about half an hour a day. Anyone who has had a baby at home will believe it.

People with a disability sleep longer, though diaries can't say whether that rest is better.

4 · Habits and work

What we do before bed follows us into the next day

If you're still working in the last hour before bed, you're in the group with almost twice the sleep problems. Late-night scrolling and six caffeinated drinks a day show the same pattern.

Nearly 1 in 3 workers made mistakes because they were too sleepy, and about 1 in 6 adults drive drowsy every week.

5 · Sleep and wellbeing

Poor health and poor sleep travel together

People living with both back pain and a mental health condition have nearly 5 bad nights a week. For people with neither, it's fewer than 3. They have about four times the rate of insomniaOngoing trouble falling asleep or staying asleep that affects how you feel the next day. and three times the rate of sleep apnoeaA condition where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, often with loud snoring..

So which comes first, the bad nights or the bad days? Probably both: worry keeps people awake, and poor sleep makes anxiety and depression worse in turn (Alvaro et al., 2013).

1 in 7

Australian adults report high or very high psychological distress.
By morning the question changes. Not how long we slept, but how we're doing.

6 · Where wellbeing is lowest

Distress runs highest in the regions, but most distressed people live in the cities

The sleep surveys used here can't be broken down by region, so these maps show psychological distressFeeling anxious, depressed or overwhelmed, measured with the K10 survey. "High or very high" means a score of 22 or more out of 50., not sleep: where people feel worst, not whether poor sleep is the cause.

Why distress belongs in a sleep story

Top reasons Australians give for sleeping poorly, Sleep Health Foundation, 2026

  • 41% a busy mind
  • 36% worry or anxiety
  • 30% restlessness

Distress ranges from 8.8% of adults in Northern Sydney to over 17% in Darling Downs and West Moreton (Qld).

Hover over a region to see the rates for women and men.

Outside Sydney, distress is 3.6 points higher than in the city, and most states show the same gap between the capital and the regions.

Brisbane and the rest of Queensland are level, and Hobart is the exception, higher than the rest of Tasmania.

Zoom in and the gaps grow. In NSW, distress runs from 7.5% in Ku-ring-gai to almost 20% in Broken Hill.

In Brighton, just north of Hobart, it's almost 1 in 3 adults (31%).

The gap is smallest in Sydney (about 2.5 points) and largest in Darling Downs and West Moreton (about 7). Women also have more bad nights: 3.5 a week, compared with 2.9 for men (2025).

A high rate in a small town is still a small number of people. Two in three distressed adults (1.76 million of 2.7 million) live in the capital cities.

7 · Who sleeps best?

What good sleepers have in common

  • Older adults They are the most likely to say they get enough sleep and wake up refreshed.
  • No baby at home Adults without a baby sleep up to 40 minutes a day more than new parents, and non-carers about 10 minutes more than carers.
  • A calm last hour No work and fewer screens before bed go with fewer sleep problems.
  • Good health People without chronic pain or a mental health condition sleep longer and more regularly.

About this story: what, why and how

What (the data). Time-use diaries from the ABS (2020–21 and 2024) and the OECD; Sleep Health Foundation surveys (2016, 2025 and 2026); modelled regional estimates of psychological distress from PHIDU (2022); and map boundaries for countries, states and Primary Health Networks. The data mixes quantities (minutes, percentages, counts), categories (sex, life circumstance, health condition), ordered values (age group, hour of day) and places (country, state, region).

Why (the purpose). For a general Australian audience: compare Australia's sleep with other countries, see when Australians sleep across the day, find out who sleeps less and which habits go with poor sleep, relate sleep to health, and locate where wellbeing is lowest.

How (the design). Advanced idioms: a waffle chart drawn as an apartment block, one window per 1 in 100 adults (share sleeping under 7 hours), a radial (polar) bar chart and a ridgeline plot (time of day), a heatmap (sleep by health condition), a box plot with dots (spread of distress between local areas), and four map idioms: choropleth maps (world and regions, with city insets and small multiples), an isoband map (sunrise times), a proportional symbol map linked to a bar chart (number of distressed adults) and a tile-grid map (capital cities against the rest of each state). Bars, dumbbells and a line chart compare groups. Colour is used sparingly: orange marks the group or time each chart points to and blue what it is compared with, pink and blue mean women and men, and purple means distress. The page moves from night to morning to carry the story from sleep to wellbeing.
