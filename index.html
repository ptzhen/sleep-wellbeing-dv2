Sleep and Wellbeing in Australia

Most of us know we should get 7 to 9 hours a night. Who actually does, what gets in the way, and how is it linked to the way Australians feel?

1 · How we compare

On paper, Australians sleep well

Averaged over a whole day, Australians spend about 8 hours 32 minutes asleep or in bed trying to sleep. That puts Australia in the middle of the OECD. Adults in Japan get about an hour less, and in Korea about 40 minutes less.

In most countries women sleep a few minutes longer than men. Australia's 2006 OECD figures follow that pattern, but in the newer 2020–21 ABS survey men slept slightly longer.

Time-use surveys count all time spent sleeping, including naps and time in bed, so these figures are higher than the hours people report in sleep surveys.

2 · A day in Australia

The weekday alarm clock steals sleep we try to win back at the weekend

By 3 am almost everyone is asleep. The difference is in the morning: between 7 and 8 am on a weekday only 43% are still asleep, while at the weekend 60% are.

That lie-in adds up to about 36 extra minutes a night. Sleep researchers call the gap "social jet lag": the body runs on one schedule and the working week on another.

Hover over an hour to see the share asleep, and switch between women and men.

The pattern is the same for women and men. Men sleep slightly longer on both kinds of day.

Clocks set the start of the working day, but the sun sets the body clock. Because Queensland has no daylight saving, on the longest day Brisbane sees sunrise at 4:49 am, 51 minutes before Sydney. Perth's winter sunrise comes after 7:15 am.

Switch between summer and winter to see how far apart the states drift.

3 · Who sleeps well

Age and life stage shape how well we sleep

Young adults spend the most time asleep (more than 9 hours for 15 to 24 year olds), yet they are the least likely to say they get enough. Two in three wake up unrefreshed.

Three in four 18 to 24 year olds go online in the hour before bed nearly every night, compared with one in five people over 65.

Life circumstances matter too. Parents of children under five sleep about half an hour less than the average adult, and mothers of under-fives and women carers sleep the least of all, about 8 hours 8 minutes.

People with a disability sleep longer, often because of health conditions or medication rather than better rest.

4 · Habits and work

What we do before bed follows us into the next day

Adults who work in the hour before bed are almost twice as likely to have several sleep problems as those who never do. Nightly screens and heavy caffeine show the same pattern, though these are associations, not proof that one causes the other.

Poor sleep doesn't stay at home. Nearly 1 in 3 workers made mistakes because they were too sleepy, and about 1 in 6 Australians drive drowsy every week. Both are most common among 25 to 34 year olds.

5 · Sleep and wellbeing

Poor health and poor sleep travel together

People with both back pain and a mental health condition have nearly 5 poor nights a week, against fewer than 3 for people with neither. They are almost four times as likely to have insomnia.

The link runs both ways: pain and worry disturb sleep, and short sleep makes pain and low mood harder to manage.

6 · Where wellbeing is lowest

Distress is highest in the regions, but most distressed people live in cities

Busy minds and worry are the top two reasons Australians give for losing sleep. Rates of high psychological distress range from 8.8% in Northern Sydney to over 17% in Darling Downs and West Moreton (Qld).

Hover over a region to see the rates for women and men.

Rates tell only half the story. Because so many people live in the capitals, the largest numbers of distressed adults are in Melbourne, Sydney, Adelaide and Brisbane, plus the populous Hunter and Central Coast.

In most states, living outside the capital means higher distress. Brisbane and Hobart are the exceptions.

7 · Who sleeps best?

Healthier sleepers have a few things in common

  • Older adults are the most likely to say they get enough sleep and wake up refreshed.
  • People without young children or caring duties get about half an hour more sleep a night.
  • A calm last hour, with no work and fewer screens, goes with fewer sleep problems.
  • People without chronic pain or mental health conditions sleep longer and more regularly.

If you're one of the third of Australians not getting 7 hours, start with the last hour before bed.
