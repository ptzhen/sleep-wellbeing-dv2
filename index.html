1 · How we compare

On paper, Australians sleep well

In the latest ABS time-use survey (2024), Australians aged 15 and over recorded about 8 hours 48 minutes of sleep a day. On the OECD's comparable figures, Australia sits in the middle of the pack. Adults in Japan get about an hour less, and in Korea about 40 minutes less.

In most countries women sleep a few minutes longer than men. Australia's 2006 OECD figures follow that pattern. In the 2024 ABS survey the gap has all but closed: women and men both sleep about 8 hours 48 minutes.

Time-use diaries add up all the time people record as sleeping across the day, including naps, so these figures are higher than the hours people report in sleep surveys.

2 · A day in Australia

The weekday alarm clock steals sleep we try to win back at the weekend

By 3 am almost everyone is asleep. The difference is in the morning: between 7 and 8 am on a weekday only 43% are still asleep, while at the weekend 60% are.

In the 2024 survey that lie-in adds up to about 44 extra minutes a night, which suggests many people run short of sleep during the working week.

Press Play the night to step from 10 pm to 10 am, or drag the hour slider. Hover over an hour to see the share asleep, and switch between women and men.

Sleep is not the only part of the day that runs to the clock. On a weekday, 38% of people who do paid work have started by 7 am and 80% are working by 9 am, the same hours in which sleep drops away.

Child care peaks three times: before school, after school and at bedtime. Free time is squeezed into the evening on weekdays, peaking at 75% between 8 and 9 pm. At the weekend it spreads through the afternoon.

Women and men follow the same pattern. Women sleep about 4 minutes longer on weekdays and men about 4 minutes longer at the weekend, so the lines almost overlap.

Clocks set the start of the working day, but the sun sets the body clock. Because Queensland has no daylight saving, on the longest day Brisbane sees sunrise at 4:49 am, 51 minutes before Sydney. Perth's winter sunrise comes after 7:15 am.

Switch between summer and winter to see how far apart the states drift.

3 · Who sleeps well

Age and life stage shape how well we sleep

Young adults spend the most time asleep: just over 9 hours a day for 15 to 24 year olds in the 2024 ABS survey. Yet in the Sleep Health Foundation survey they are the least likely to say they get enough, and two in three often wake up unrefreshed.

Three in four 18 to 24 year olds go online in the hour before bed nearly every night, compared with one in five people over 65.

Life circumstances matter too. Parents whose youngest child is under one sleep about half an hour less than the average adult (8 hours 16 minutes against 8 hours 48). The gap is biggest while the youngest child is a baby, but parents of school-age children still sleep about 20 minutes less. Carers and volunteers sleep about 8 minutes less.

People with a disability spend longer asleep. Time-use data only records duration, so it can't say whether that means better rest.

4 · Habits and work

What we do before bed follows us into the next day

Adults who work in the hour before bed are almost twice as likely to have several sleep problems as those who never do. Nightly screens and heavy caffeine show the same pattern, though these are associations, not proof that one causes the other.

Poor sleep doesn't stay at home. Nearly 1 in 3 people in work made mistakes because they were too sleepy, rising to almost half of 18 to 24 year olds. About 1 in 6 adults drive drowsy every week, and nearly 1 in 3 of those aged 25 to 34.

5 · Sleep and wellbeing

Poor health and poor sleep travel together

People with both back pain and a mental health condition have nearly 5 poor nights a week, against fewer than 3 for people with neither. They are almost four times as likely to have insomnia.

The link runs both ways: pain and worry disturb sleep, and short sleep makes pain and low mood harder to manage.

6 · Where wellbeing is lowest

Where Australians feel worst: distress is highest in the regions, but most distressed people live in cities

No Australian survey measures sleep region by region, so these maps show the wellbeing side of the story instead: psychological distress, not sleep. They can show where people feel worst, but not whether poor sleep is the cause.

The link is still worth watching: a busy mind (47%) and worry (42%) are the top reasons Australians give for losing sleep. Distress ranges from 8.8% of adults in Northern Sydney to over 17% in Darling Downs and West Moreton (Qld), and outside the capitals it is higher in every state except Queensland and Tasmania.

Terms used here. Psychological distress is measured with the K10, a 10-question survey of anxiety and depression symptoms over the past four weeks; a "high or very high" score is 22 or more out of 50. Primary Health Networks (PHNs) are the 31 regions the Australian Government uses to plan local health services. Age-standardised rates adjust for differences in age mix, so a region with many older or younger people can be compared fairly.

Hover over a region to see the rates for women and men.

Splitting each state into its capital city and the rest makes the regional gap clearer. Outside Sydney, distress is 3.6 points higher than in the city (15.4% against 11.8%), and Melbourne, Darwin, Perth and Adelaide show the same pattern.

Queensland is the exception, with Brisbane and the rest of the state level, and Hobart is higher than the rest of Tasmania.

Women report more distress than men in every region. The gap is smallest in Sydney (2.4 to 2.7 points) and largest in Darling Downs and West Moreton, Tasmania and Murray (about 7 points), just as women in the 2025 sleep survey reported more poor nights (3.5 vs 2.9 a week) and more insomnia (13% vs 9%).

Rates tell only half the story. Because so many people live in the capitals, the largest numbers of distressed adults are in Melbourne, Sydney, Adelaide and Brisbane, plus the populous Hunter and Central Coast.

7 · Who sleeps best?

Healthier sleepers have a few things in common

  • Older adults They are the most likely to say they get enough sleep and wake up refreshed.
  • No baby at home Adults without a baby sleep up to 40 minutes a day more than new parents, and non-carers about 10 minutes more than carers.
  • A calm last hour No work and fewer screens before bed go with fewer sleep problems.
  • Good health People without chronic pain or a mental health condition sleep longer and more regularly.

If you're one of the third of Australians not getting 7 hours, start with the last hour before bed.
