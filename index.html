1 · How we compare

On paper, Australians sleep well

In the ABS time-use surveyPeople record what they do across a whole day in a diary, so sleep is added up from the diary rather than estimated from memory. (2024), Australians aged 15 and over recorded about 8 hours 48 minutes of sleep a day. That puts Australia mid-pack among OECDOrganisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, a group of 38 mostly high-income countries. Its time-use data also covers a few non-members, such as China, India and South Africa. countries: Japan sleeps about an hour less, and Korea about 40 minutes less.

In most countries women sleep a few minutes longer than men. Australia's 2006 OECD figures follow that pattern. In the 2024 ABS survey the gap has all but closed: women and men both sleep about 8 hours 48 minutes.

Time-use diaries add up all the time people record as sleeping across the day, including naps, so these figures are higher than the hours people report in sleep surveys.

So why do a third of adults say they sleep less than 7 hours a night?

2 · A day in Australia

The weekday alarm clock steals sleep we try to win back at the weekend

By 3 am almost everyone is asleep. The difference is in the morning: between 7 and 8 am on a weekday only 43% are still asleep, while at the weekend 60% are. In the 2024 survey that lie-in adds up to about 44 extra minutes a night, a sign that many people run short of sleep during the working week.

Press Play the night to step from 10 pm to 10 am, or drag the hour slider. Hover over an hour to see the share asleep, and switch between women and men.

Sleep is not the only part of the day that runs to the clock. On a weekday, 38% of people who do paid work have started by 7 am and 80% are working by 9 am, the same hours in which sleep drops away.

Child care peaks three times: before school, after school and at bedtime. Free time is squeezed into the evening on weekdays, peaking at 75% between 8 and 9 pm. At the weekend it spreads through the afternoon.

Clocks set the start of the working day, but the sun sets the body clock. Because Queensland has no daylight saving, on the longest day Brisbane sees sunrise at 4:49 am, 51 minutes before Sydney. Perth's winter sunrise comes after 7:15 am. Switch between summer and winter to compare.

3 · Who sleeps well

Age and life stage shape how well we sleep

Young adults spend the most time asleep: just over 9 hours a day for 15 to 24 year olds in the 2024 ABS survey. Yet in the Sleep Health Foundation survey they are the least likely to say they get enough, and two in three often wake up unrefreshed.

Three in four 18 to 24 year olds go online in the hour before bed nearly every night, compared with one in five people over 65.

A baby costs the most sleep: parents whose youngest child is under one get about half an hour less than the average adult (8 hours 16 minutes against 8 hours 48). Parents of school-age children still lose about 20 minutes, and carers and volunteers about 8.

People with a disability spend longer asleep. Time-use data only records duration, so it can't say whether that means better rest.

4 · Habits and work

What we do before bed follows us into the next day

Adults who work in the hour before bed are almost twice as likely to have several sleep problems as those who never do. Nightly screens and heavy caffeine show the same pattern, though these are associations, not proof that one causes the other.

Poor sleep doesn't stay at home. Nearly 1 in 3 people in work made mistakes because they were too sleepy, rising to almost half of 18 to 24 year olds. About 1 in 6 adults drive drowsy every week, and nearly 1 in 3 of those aged 25 to 34.

5 · Sleep and wellbeing

Poor health and poor sleep travel together

People with both back pain and a mental health condition have nearly 5 poor nights a week, against fewer than 3 for people with neither. They are almost four times as likely to have insomniaOngoing trouble falling asleep or staying asleep that affects how you feel the next day. and three times as likely to report sleep apnoeaA condition where breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, often with loud snoring..

The link runs both ways: pain and worry disturb sleep, and short sleep makes pain and low mood harder to manage.

6 · Where wellbeing is lowest

Where Australians feel worst: distress is highest in the regions, but most distressed people live in cities

No Australian survey measures sleep region by region, so these maps show the wellbeing side of the story instead: psychological distressFeeling anxious, depressed or overwhelmed, measured with the K10 survey. "High or very high" means a score of 22 or more out of 50., not sleep. They can show where people feel worst, but not whether poor sleep is the cause.

Why distress belongs in a sleep story

Top reasons Australians give for losing sleep, 2025

  • 47% a busy mind
  • 42% worry or anxiety
  • 31% restlessness

Distress ranges from 8.8% of adults in Northern Sydney to over 17% in Darling Downs and West Moreton (Qld).

Hover over a region to see the rates for women and men.

Splitting each state into its capital city and the rest makes the regional gap clearer. Outside Sydney, distress is 3.6 points higher than in the city (15.4% against 11.8%), and Melbourne, Darwin, Perth and Adelaide show the same pattern.

Queensland is the exception, with Brisbane and the rest of the state level, and Hobart is higher than the rest of Tasmania.

State averages hide a lot. In four states the most distressed local area has more than twice the rate of the least, and in NSW it ranges from 7.5% in Ku-ring-gai on Sydney's North Shore to almost 20% in Broken Hill.

Tasmania has the highest typical area, and Brighton, north of Hobart, stands out at 31%.

The gap is smallest in Sydney (about 2.5 points) and largest in Darling Downs and West Moreton, Tasmania and Murray (about 7). It echoes the 2025 sleep survey, where women reported more poor nights (3.5 vs 2.9 a week) and more insomnia (13% vs 9%).

Rates tell only half the story. Because so many people live in the capitals, the largest numbers of distressed adults are in Melbourne, Sydney, Adelaide and Brisbane, plus the populous Hunter and Central Coast.

7 · Who sleeps best?

Healthier sleepers have a few things in common

  • Older adults They are the most likely to say they get enough sleep and wake up refreshed.
  • No baby at home Adults without a baby sleep up to 40 minutes a day more than new parents, and non-carers about 10 minutes more than carers.
  • A calm last hour No work and fewer screens before bed go with fewer sleep problems.
  • Good health People without chronic pain or a mental health condition sleep longer and more regularly.

If you're one of the third of Australians not getting 7 hours, start with the last hour before bed.

About this story: what, why and how

What (the data). Time-use diaries from the ABS (2020–21 and 2024) and the OECD; two Sleep Health Foundation surveys (2016 and 2025); modelled regional estimates of psychological distress from PHIDU (2022); and map boundaries for countries, states and Primary Health Networks. The data mixes quantities (minutes, percentages, counts), categories (sex, life circumstance, health condition), ordered values (age group, hour of day) and places (country, state, region).

Why (the purpose). For a general Australian audience: compare Australia's sleep with other countries, see when Australians sleep across the day, find out who sleeps less and which habits go with poor sleep, relate sleep to health, and locate where wellbeing is lowest.

How (the design). Advanced idioms: a waffle chart (share sleeping under 7 hours), a radial (polar) bar chart and a ridgeline plot (time of day), a heatmap (sleep by health condition), a box plot with dots (spread of distress between local areas), and four map idioms: choropleth maps (world and regions, with city insets and small multiples), an isoband map (sunrise times), a proportional symbol map linked to a bar chart (number of distressed adults) and a tile-grid map (capital cities against the rest of each state). Bars, dumbbells and a line chart compare groups. Colour is used sparingly: orange marks the group or time each chart points to and blue what it is compared with, pink and blue mean women and men, and purple means distress. The page moves from night to morning to carry the story from sleep to wellbeing.
